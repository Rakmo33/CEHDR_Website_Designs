SLIDE 2
45 seconds. Blank right side for household planning image. Begin with a family emergency plan. Write down emergency contacts, including someone outside your local area. Choose a meeting location and make sure everyone knows how to reach it. Discuss evacuation and sheltering in place. Evacuation means leaving an unsafe area. Sheltering in place means staying inside when authorities tell you that is safer. Follow official instructions, and account for transportation, mobility, and caregiving needs. Sign up for local emergency alerts. Keep important phone numbers on paper. Agree on how you will reconnect if phones or internet service are unavailable. Review the plan together before an emergency happens.

SLIDE 3
90 seconds. Right half stays blank for the exact CEHDR backpack. Animate separate item images beside it in Canva. Your preparedness kit should bring the essentials together in one accessible place. Start with water and nonperishable food that your household can use. Consider dietary needs, and include a manual can opener when needed. Add a flashlight so you can move safely if the power goes out. A battery-powered radio can help you hear official updates when other services fail. Pack spare batteries that fit your equipment, or portable power for the devices you depend on. Include first-aid supplies. Make room for medications and the information needed to use them correctly. Add personal identification, emergency contacts, and copies of important documents. Keep these protected from water. Think about the supplies that are specific to your household, such as mobility, hearing, vision, or other medical needs. Include familiar comfort items. As you gather each item, ask: Who will use it? Can they use it easily? Is anything missing? The kit should support the people in your home, rather than simply repeat a generic checklist. Keep your plan and key information with the kit so you can take them together when you need to leave.

SLIDE 4
60 seconds. Right side blank for African American older adult and caregiver. Every household has different needs. Keep a current medication list, and plan with a pharmacist or care team for several days of medication when possible. Include caregiver and emergency contacts, and copies of identification. Consider mobility aids, hearing-aid batteries, glasses, and other medical supplies the person relies on. For someone living with dementia, unfamiliar places and disrupted routines can be upsetting. Add familiar comfort items and simple information about their routines, communication preferences, and care needs. Include the name and phone number of a caregiver who can help. Think about what keeps the person calm, how they communicate discomfort, and what support they need to move safely. Review these details with the person and their care team where possible. Personalizing the kit now can make an emergency less confusing for everyone.

SLIDE 5
30 seconds. Right side blank for kit near an exit. Store your kit where you can reach it quickly, and make sure everyone in the household knows where it is. Review it every six months, and whenever health or caregiving needs change. Check expiration dates and replace supplies as needed. Follow storage instructions for food, water, batteries, and medications. Update contact details and medication lists. A kit only helps when its contents are ready to use.
