New Measure · First of Its Kind
Functional Religious Attendance

This study introduced a brand-new measure — never used before in caregiving research. Functional religious attendance captures not just whether Black caregivers attend services, but whether caregiving responsibilities get in the way. It separates choosing not to go from being unable to go — a distinction that is especially critical for overburdened communities and for understanding how access to cultural coping resources shapes health outcomes.

2
💡
Surprising Finding
Wanting to Go Isn't the Same as Going

Caregiving didn't weaken Black caregivers' faith — it blocked their ability to act on it. Role overload reduced actual attendance at religious services, but not how much caregivers valued going. And only actually being present was linked to better sleep. Valuing religion alone offered no significant protection. This challenges a long-held assumption in both research and practice: that believing in the importance of faith is enough to buffer stress. Without access, the protection disappears.

3
🤝
Call to Action
Faith Communities Must Come to the Caregiver

When caregivers lose access to their faith community, they lose far more than worship — they lose fellowship, emotional support, and restoration. Some Black caregivers reported feeling abandoned by their church once they could no longer attend. Faith organizations, healthcare providers, and policymakers must close this gap: offer flexible and virtual services, actively reach out to absent congregants, and fund community-based caregiver support. Dementia-friendly churches are already showing the way. The support must go to the caregiver.

📌
The bottom line: Caregiving stress reshapes daily life, relationships, and access to the very resources that protect health. Addressing sleep disparities among Black caregivers requires recognizing these layered burdens — and designing systems, communities, and research that meet caregivers where they actually are.
