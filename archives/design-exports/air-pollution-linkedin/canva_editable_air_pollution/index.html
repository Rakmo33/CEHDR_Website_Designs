Air Pollution and Mental Health

Among African Americans and Caribbean Blacks:

Findings from the National Survey of American Life

What if we've been underestimating the mental health consequences of air pollution because we've overlooked the populations most affected?

A new study led by Dr. Karen D. Lincoln at the University of California, Irvine explores the relationship between fine particulate air pollution—PM2.5—and mental health among African Americans and Caribbean Blacks.

This study represents several important scientific advances.

First, it is the first study to integrate national air pollution exposure data with the National Survey of American Life (NSAL), one of the most comprehensive studies of Black Americans ever conducted. Linking these datasets created a unique opportunity to examine how environmental exposures influence mental health in populations that have been largely absent from environmental epidemiology.

Second, to our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the relationship between air pollution and mental health among Black Americans. It is also the only study to investigate this relationship among Caribbean Black adults, a population that is almost entirely absent from the environmental health literature.

Our findings showed that higher PM2.5 exposure was associated with greater odds of lifetime anxiety disorders among Caribbean Black adults. But the implications extend far beyond air pollution alone.

This research is grounded in the stress–exposure disease framework, which recognizes that environmental exposures interact with chronic social stressors to shape health. Air pollution is not experienced in isolation. It is experienced within riskscapes. Unequal environments produced by structural racism, where multiple hazards cluster together, including neighborhood disinvestment, traffic, noise, housing insecurity, economic hardship, and discrimination. These cumulative exposures create conditions that increase vulnerability to disease across the life course.

Findings also demonstrate why Black populations should not be treated as a single, homogeneous group. African Americans and Caribbean Black adults have distinct historical, cultural, and migration experiences that shape environmental exposures and their consequences for mental health.

Health equity will not be achieved by focusing on individual risk factors alone. We must understand and transform the structurally produced riskscapes that drive cumulative exposure, chronic stress, and disease.
