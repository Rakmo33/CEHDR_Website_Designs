What Is This Guide For?

This guide supports the Say It Loud citizen panel process in identifying the most trusted and central community voices to serve on the Community Equity Board (CEB). Rather than selecting CEB members through top-down researcher decisions, Say It Loud uses virtual power mapping — a community-driven tool — to ensure the people who govern this research truly represent the communities most affected by Alzheimer's Disease and Related Dementias (ADRD).

Say It Loud Goal Through the virtual power mapping process, citizen panelists identify the most central, trusted, and influential community members to invite to join the CEB (no more than 8 members total). This keeps community governance authentic and prevents the CEB from becoming just another advisory committee selected by researchers.

Four Types of Power

Before beginning power mapping, it is important for everyone to share the same understanding of what power means. Power is not only negative — it can be a force for healing, justice, and community strength.

Power Over

Authority, control, or domination. Institutions or people who control others' behavior, access, resources, or opportunities — often through systems that have been in place so long they feel invisible.

Power To

The ability to act and make change. Starts with believing change is possible and grows as people take action, build skills, and see results. This is the power we are trying to activate in communities.

Power With

Collective action and solidarity. The strength that comes from people joining together, building shared understanding, and acting as one. This is community organizing power.

Power Within

Self-worth, dignity, and confidence. The internal sense that your voice matters and that you can affect the world around you. This is foundational to authentic community engagement.

Connection to Say It Loud Say It Loud is built on the belief that authentic ADRD research with Black Americans requires all four types of power to be present in the research process. The CEB is designed to hold Power Over the study design through its veto authority. Power mapping is designed to build Power With and Power Within the citizen panels by making the governance process visible and community-driven.

Learning Objectives for Co-Facilitators

By the end of this training, community co-facilitators will be able to:

  1. Explain in plain language what virtual power mapping is and why Say It Loud uses it
  2. Lead a group of citizen panelists through the four types of power discussion over video call
  3. Introduce and explain the two-axis power map without relying on research language
  4. Guide panelists through the stakeholder brainstorm and digital placement process
  5. Keep the activity grounded in panelists' own community knowledge — not researcher assumptions
  6. Identify which completed map quadrant points toward CEB candidates

Key Concepts to Teach

1. The Two-Axis Map

The power map uses two lines to place every community stakeholder in one of four positions:

  • X-axis (left to right): From strongly opposed to the community's goals → to strongly supportive
  • Y-axis (bottom to top): From little influence in the community → to a lot of influence

The upper right quadrant — high power, strongly supportive — is where your best CEB candidates live. The upper left — high power, opposed or uncertain — are people to engage and potentially move.

2. Naming Names

Power maps are strongest when they name specific individuals and organizations, not just types. Teach co-facilitators to gently push panelists to be specific: not "church leaders" but "Pastor Jones at First Baptist" or "Deacon Williams from New Life Church." This makes the map actionable.

If a panelist is hesitant to name someone, reassure them that the power map stays within the group. It is a community planning tool, not a public document. Names on the map are held in confidence by the citizen panel.

3. Co-Creating the Map

The map belongs to the panelists, not the researcher or co-facilitator. The co-facilitator's job is to screen share, ask questions, and update fields based on consensus — not to tell panelists where someone belongs on the map. If panelists disagree about placement, that is a healthy sign of a living community. Let them discuss it.

4. The Map Is Never Finished

Power shifts over time. The power map done in Session 1 is a starting point, not a final answer. The CEB may revisit and update the map as the study progresses and new community relationships develop.

Facilitation Scripts for Co-Facilitators

These are suggested ways to introduce and explain key concepts. Co-facilitators should use their own voice and not read these word-for-word.

Opening Power Conversation

When we talk about power, a lot of us think about someone having power over us — a boss, a landlord, a doctor who doesn't listen. But there are other kinds of power too. The power we have inside ourselves. The power we build when we come together. And the power to make something happen. Today we're going to look at all of those kinds of power — who has them in our community and how we can use that to make sure the right people are guiding this research.

Introducing the Map

We're going to make a map together — not of streets, but of people and organizations in our community. I am sharing my screen so we can watch it develop in real time. This map has two directions. Left and right tells us how much someone supports what we're trying to do here — making sure Black Americans have a real voice in brain health research. Up and down tells us how much influence they have in the community. Someone who is high up on this map and all the way to the right is someone we want involved in leading this work. Those are the people we're looking for.

Naming Names

I want us to get specific. Instead of just saying "community leaders" or "doctors," let's name the actual people we know. Who is that pastor? Who is that nurse at the health center? Who is the person everyone calls when something needs to get done? This map is just for us — it's not going anywhere. The more specific we are, the more useful it is.

Handling Common Challenges

Challenge 1 — Panelists disagree about where to place someone Disagreement is valuable, not a problem. Say: "It sounds like you all have different experiences with this person. Let's hear both sides." Allow the discussion. Then ask the group to come to a placement together or note both perspectives on the screen. Different views reveal the complexity of community power.
Challenge 2 — Panelists are reluctant to name people who have harmed the community Some panelists may hesitate to name people in the upper-left quadrant (high power, opposing). Acknowledge this: "We don't have to name anyone who makes anyone uncomfortable. If there's someone we all know is not on our side, we can just describe the role without the name for now." The map can still be useful even with some anonymous entries.
Challenge 3 — Panelists name the same few people and get stuck Use prompting questions: "Who do people call when something goes wrong in this neighborhood? Who shows up at community meetings? Who do young people trust? Who do elders trust? Who at the hospital actually listens?" Different entry points unlock different networks.
Challenge 4 — Panelists ask if researchers will see the map Be clear and honest: "Dr. Lincoln will see the final list of people we identify as potential CEB members so those invitations can be sent. But the map itself — who we placed where and why — stays in our group. We are in control of this process." This reinforces the community governance principle at the heart of Say It Loud.

What Dr. Lincoln Does During the Panel Session

Zero Researcher Presence for Condition A Panels For Condition A citizen panels (Empower), Dr. Lincoln does not attend the virtual power mapping session. Community co-facilitators lead the full activity independently over video call. Dr. Lincoln reviews the completed CEB candidate list — not the map itself — after the session. This is a deliberate application of the Say It Loud community autonomy principle.

For Condition B panels (Collaborate), Dr. Lincoln may attend the call as a resource but should not direct the conversation or place stakeholders on the shared screen. The co-facilitator leads. Dr. Lincoln speaks only when directly asked, and defers to panelist knowledge above research expertise on all questions about community relationships.

Step-by-Step Instructions

⏱ 5 min

Step 1 — Welcome and Check-In

Welcome everyone back and remind them why this virtual session matters.

Welcome everyone. Today we're doing something a little different. We're going to make a map together on screen — not of neighborhoods, but of people. We want to figure out who in our community should be at the table helping guide this research. Nobody knows that better than you do. So today, your knowledge is the most important thing on this call.
Start with a brief check-in question that grounds everyone in the community. Try: "What is one word that describes your neighborhood right now?" Have participants unmute or share in the chat.
⏱ 15 min

Step 2 — What Is Power? Group Conversation

Before mapping power, the group needs to talk about what power means. This is not a lecture — it is an open call conversation.

Before we start the map, I want to ask you something: when you hear the word "power," what comes to mind? What does power look like in your neighborhood?

Let the group respond freely. Listen for examples of each type of power and name them when you hear them:

  • If someone mentions a landlord, city official, or hospital — that is power over.
  • If someone mentions organizing, a community meeting, or a coalition — that is power with.
  • If someone mentions a neighbor who gets things done — that is power to.
  • If someone mentions feeling heard, respected, or like their voice matters — that is power within.
All of those are real kinds of power. Today we're going to look at the people in our community who have power — and figure out who we want to bring into this research to make sure it serves us.
⏱ 10 min

Step 3 — Introduce the Power Map

Share your screen displaying the large canvas with the axes already drawn. Walk the group through what the map means.

This is our power map. It has two directions. This line going left and right — that's about whether someone supports what we're doing. All the way to the right means they're all in, they want to help. All the way to the left means they're not on our side, at least not yet. The line going up and down is about how much influence they have. High up means they have a lot of pull in the community. Low down means they're less connected to how things work. We're going to put people and organizations on this map. We want to find the ones who are up high AND on the right side — those are our people.
⏱ 10 min

Step 4 — Individual Stakeholder Brainstorm Review

Have panelists reference the pre-session brainstorm sheets they prepared either on paper or via the online form link.

Before we start putting names on the map together, let's open up the notes you prepared during our pre-session activity. Think about the individuals and organizations you listed out as holding real influence, trust, or connection in your neighborhood. We will go around the call and share those ideas to build our digital map together.
If a panelist didn't get a chance to complete the activity beforehand, gently offer: "Take a minute right now to think about who you would call if something went wrong in your neighborhood, or who you would trust to speak up for your family, and we will add them."
⏱ 25 min

Step 5 — Group Discussion and Map Placement

Now bring the group together to share and place stakeholders on the shared digital map.

Let's share what we came up with. Who wants to start? Tell us the name — or describe the person or organization — and where you think they belong on our screen. Does everyone agree? Let's talk about it.

Use these questions to guide the discussion and keep it moving:

  • Who are the types of individuals or organizations that have a voice in brain health and health research in our community?
  • Who is directly affected by ADRD in our community? Who do they turn to?
  • Who supports the idea that Black communities should lead their own health research?
  • Who might push back on that — or who hasn't been convinced yet?
  • Who is missing from this screen that should be on it?

As names are shared, type them onto the digital sticky notes and position them on the shared canvas. Use one color for individuals and another for organizations. If two panelists disagree on placement, discuss it and find a shared position — or note both views.

Be specific. If someone says "church leaders," ask: Which church? Which leader? The more specific the map, the more useful it is. Name names.
⏱ 10 min

Step 6 — Identify CEB Candidates Together

Look at the completed map on screen together and guide the group toward identifying candidates for the Community Equity Board.

Now let's look at what we made. See this area up here and to the right? These are the people who have real influence in our community AND who are on our side — who believe that our community deserves to lead this research. These are the people we want to invite to the Community Equity Board. This is a small group — no more than 8 people. So we need to choose wisely. Who stands out to you? Who would bring something to this table that no one else could?

Guide the group through the CEB candidate criteria on screen. Aim to identify 8–12 names as potential candidates, knowing that some may decline the invitation.

⏱ 5 min

Step 7 — Closing Reflection

You just did something powerful. You mapped the power in your own community using your own knowledge over this call — no researcher needed to tell you who matters here. The names you came up with today are going to shape who governs this entire research study. That is real influence. Thank you for bringing your full selves to this virtual meeting.

Stakeholder Brainstorm Prompts

Use these questions to help panelists generate names for the map. Ask them one at a time over the call and give the group time to respond to each before moving on.

Community Influence and Trust

  • Who do people call when something goes wrong in this neighborhood?
  • Who speaks and people actually listen?
  • Who do elders in this community trust?
  • Who do young people trust?
  • Who shows up consistently — at funerals, at graduations, at community gatherings?

Health and Caregiving

  • Who helps families navigate health care in this community?
  • Who do you see at church health fairs, at the senior center, at the barbershop or beauty shop?
  • Who talks openly about memory loss, dementia, or caring for aging parents?
  • Who do families call when they don't know what to do about a family member's brain health?

Organizations and Institutions

  • Which organizations serve Black seniors or Black families in this area?
  • Which churches, mosques, or faith communities are active in community health?
  • Which community organizations have real relationships with Black residents — not just service delivery?
  • Which hospitals, health centers, or clinics have earned community trust?

Decision-Makers and Systems

  • Who on the city council, school board, or in county government actually hears from this community?
  • Who at the health department, housing authority, or social services has made decisions that helped or hurt this neighborhood?

Research and Knowledge

  • Who has been part of a health study or clinical trial? Who encouraged others to participate or discouraged them?
  • Who in this community knows the most about brain health, caregiving, or medical research?
  • Who has a personal or family experience with Alzheimer's Disease or a related condition?

Clusters and Networks

After placing individuals and organizations on the shared screen layout, look for patterns. Are there clusters of sticky notes grouped together? These clusters often represent networks of influence that move together.

  • Draw digital boundary loops around clusters that appear to be connected to each other.
  • Use lines or link markers on your canvas to show connections between individuals and organizations you know have a relationship.
  • Ask: What organizations are already well-placed to affect this issue? How can this research support them — and how can they support this research?
From the Power Mapping Framework "In some cases, you might see a cluster of individuals and organizations. We want to focus on connecting the dots between these clusters to understand the power relationships among the networks." Networks matter as much as individuals in community governance.
The Goal The CEB will include no more than 8 community members who hold veto authority over the Say It Loud study design, recruitment, and the use of research findings. These are not advisors — they are decision-makers. The power mapping process exists to ensure the right people are in that role: those with community trust, community knowledge, and alignment with the goal of community-led ADRD research.

CEB Candidate Criteria

After completing the power map, use these criteria to evaluate potential CEB candidates from the upper-right quadrant. Not everyone in that quadrant will be the right fit. Panelists should discuss each name over the call using these dimensions.

What Makes a Strong CEB Member?

🤝
Community Trust: People in this community know and trust this person. They would accept a call or an invitation from them.
🧠
Lived or Professional Knowledge of ADRD: They have personal caregiving experience, professional health background, or deep community knowledge about brain health and aging in Black communities.
📣
Voice and Presence: They speak up when it matters. They will not be silent in a virtual room with researchers and health professionals.
🌍
Community Connection: They are actively present in the community — not just known by name but engaged in its day-to-day life.
⚖️
Commitment to Equity: They believe Black communities deserve power over research that affects them. This is non-negotiable for CEB governance.
🗓
Availability: They have capacity to participate. The CEB meets regularly over video conference. Someone who cannot attend cannot govern effectively.
🌈
Diversity of Perspective: The CEB should not all come from the same network, age group, faith community, or part of the region. Different perspectives make better decisions.
🏅
Independence: Ideally, CEB members are not direct employees of AAAE or the research team. They represent the community, not the study.

CEB Selection Process — Step by Step

1

Review the Upper-Right Quadrant Together

Ask the group: "Looking at everyone in this corner of our shared screen — high power, supportive — who stands out as the strongest candidates for the CEB? Who would you trust to make decisions about this research on behalf of this community?"

2

Apply the Criteria

For each top candidate, discuss the eight criteria above as a group over the video call. No formal scoring is needed — the conversation itself will reveal who rises to the top through consensus.

3

Check for Diversity

Before finalizing the list, ask: "Are we missing someone? Are all of our candidates from the same church, the same neighborhood, the same age group? Who else should be at this table?" Aim for 2 members per county (Riverside County, Los Angeles County, Orange County) with representation of elders, caregivers, and community organizers.

4

Generate a Final List of 8–12 Names

Aim for more names than seats — some people will decline the invitation. Record names, contact information if available, and a brief note on why this person was identified by the panel. This summary sheet list goes to the project leaders for outreach.

5

The Invitation Belongs to the Community

Wherever possible, the invitation to join the CEB should come from a citizen panelist, not from the research team. A call from a trusted community member is more likely to result in a yes than an email from a researcher. Panelists who know the candidates personally should be the ones to make the initial ask.

What Happens After the Map

The Map Stays With the Community The completed power map canvas is a community document. A summary of CEB candidates — names and brief rationale — is shared with project leadership to coordinate invitations. The full map layout, including all stakeholder placements and discussion notes, remains confidential to the citizen panel. This is a deliberate governance decision that reflects the Say It Loud principle of community ownership of the research process.

After the virtual power mapping session, the co-facilitator should:

  1. Complete the CEB Candidate Summary Sheet with names and brief notes from the session
  2. Export or screenshot the completed power map layout for the panel's own records
  3. Deliver the CEB Candidate Summary Sheet directly to project leadership
  4. Follow up at the next video session to report back to panelists on which candidates have been contacted and who has agreed to serve
Keep the loop closed. Panelists invested their knowledge and trust in this process. Reporting back on who joined the CEB — and giving credit to the panel for identifying them — reinforces the community governance principle and builds continued trust in the research process.
